## A2: natural ("interpolated") apogee and perigee, ids 21 and 22

**Definition.** Swiss docs §2.2.4 follows Koch ("Was ist Lilith…", Meridian 1/95), Koch &
Rindgen ("Lilith und Priapus", 2000) and Revilla ("The Astronomical Variants of the Lunar
Apogee"). At its real passages through apogee or perigee, the Moon *is* the natural
apogee or perigee. Between passages the point follows a smooth curve "derived from the
analytical lunar theory", not a numerical interpolation.

**Implementation** (`points/intp.rs`). At time t, keep the arguments of the lunar theory
that do not belong to the Moon's own motion: Sun, planets, and the secular perigee and
node. Move only the Moon along its orbit: W1 → W1 + Δ, so D, F, l and ζ all shift by Δ.

- **Choice of Δ.** Δ makes the true time derivative of the distance vanish:
  Σ ∂r/∂argᵢ · ṡᵢ = 0, with l + Δ near 0 (perigee) or π (apogee). This is "the apsis the
  Moon would be passing if it were there now".
- **Theory.** Longitude, latitude and distance of that virtual passage come from
  ELP 2000-82B (Chapront-Touzé & Chapront 1983, 1988; constants fitted to DE200/LE200):
  main problem plus Earth- and Moon-figure, planetary, tidal, relativistic and
  solar-eccentricity perturbations, truncated at |A|·20^k > 1e-7 rad. That is 918 terms
  in `points/elp_main.rs`, generated by `tools/gen_intp_series.py` from the authors' files
  (see "ELP 2000-82B source" below). The theory is used in its native form: the authors'
  mean arguments and DE200 constant corrections, full polynomial arguments for the main
  problem and linear arguments for the perturbations, as in their routine ELP82B_2. Only
  the offset of the virtual apsis from ELP's mean perigee W2 is taken from the theory;
  it is added to the mean apsis line of date (published ELP/MPP02 W2 + IAU 2006 p_A,
  as for mean Lilith). The W2 polynomials of the two theories differ by 0.054″ per
  century, under 0.0003° over 0–2400.
- **Passages.** When the Moon is really at an apsis, Δ = 0 and the point is the Moon. At
  the actual passages of the JPL DE441 Moon, the curve lies within 0.001–0.0015° rms of
  the JPL Moon in 1000–2400 and 0.004° at year 0. Latitude agrees to 0.0001–0.0005° and
  distance to 0.1–0.8 km.

Output is in the mean ecliptic of date, with Δψ added for the true equinox (like the other
lunar points). Speeds are numerical. No ephemeris access is needed.

**What was tested to find the construction Swiss uses** (black-box, over years 0–2400):

- **Extremum condition.**
  - ∂r/∂Δ = 0 (the naive "virtual passage") is 0.29° (apogee) and 1.8° (perigee) rms
    from Swiss.
  - Shifting only l, or l with D or F, is 0.1–24° rms from Swiss.
  - Keeping the Sun fixed in the rate vector is 0.06° rms from Swiss.
  - The true-rate condition is the only one that reproduces the documented behaviour.
- **Lunar model.** Swiss's natural apsides at the real passages are *not* the Moon's
  place. Swiss's own Moon matches JPL to 0.0001° at those instants, yet its 21/22 there
  differ from it by 0.004° sd. The difference has a decade-scale drifting mean of
  ±0.006°.
  - So Swiss does not interpolate through actual passages of its Moon.
  - It evaluates a theory curve. The documentation says it is derived from Moshier's
    analytical lunar theory (ELP 2000-85 based).
- **ELP main problem only.** With the main problem alone (an earlier version, 466
  terms), the curve is 0.023° (apogee) and 0.045° (perigee) rms from Swiss at year 0.
  Adding the perturbation series brings this to 0.005° and 0.007° in every era.
- **Choice of lunar theory.** An earlier version used ELP/MPP02 (931 terms). It was
  replaced by ELP 2000-82B, available from IMCCE in the authors' original files. The
  agreement with Swiss is the same within 0.003° (table below). ELP 2000-82B is also
  closer in lineage to Swiss's basis, Moshier's adaptation of ELP 2000-85.
- **JPL anchoring.** Adding JPL − theory at the four nearest JPL passages (the previous
  version) now makes the agreement *worse*: 0.0065° vs 0.0050° rms at year 0. It was
  dropped, consistent with Swiss not passing through the passages.
- **What remains.** The residual is a term with the period of the anomalistic month
  (27.545 d), amplitude about 0.004°, plus sidebands at 27.24 and 27.85 d. It depends
  on the Moon's actual mean anomaly at t, which a pure virtual-apsis construction
  cannot contain. It must come from a detail of Koch's undocumented discretisation in
  Swiss. No published description of that detail was found, so it is not modelled.

## A3: hypothetical bodies

Each body is an unperturbed Kepler orbit (`fictitious.rs`):

- Heliocentric orbits use the Gaussian constant. Geocentric ones use GM(Earth).
- The mean motion comes from a, which is the Swiss documented convention, unless the
  source gives the motion explicitly.
- Elements are referred to the mean ecliptic and equinox of the stated epoch, rotated to
  GCRS with the engine's precession.
- Positions then go through the planet pipeline: light time, deflection, aberration,
  frames. `ephem::apparent_with` is `apparent` with the target given as a function.

**Interpretation choices** (each is the reading that reproduces the documented Swiss
behaviour; no number was fitted):

- **Uranians.** Neely's Table I gives `NODE = Ω₀ + 1.3960T` (the general precession) and
  explicit mean-anomaly rates.
  - Fixed equinox 1900 with no node term and Keplerian mean motion matches Swiss to
    < 1e-4°.
  - Equinox of date with +1.396T gives up to 0.2° in latitude at year 0.
  - Neely's printed rates are Keplerian to < 1e-4 relative (unit test), but they give
    0.05° at year 0.
- **Kronos.** Neely prints A = 64.81690 and M = 169.0193 + 68.98746T. I checked the scan:
  the rate is consistent with that A. Swiss's Kronos behaves as if its orbit were about
  0.003 AU larger, so the difference grows linearly to 0.08° at year 0. We keep the
  published value. Neely's earlier version (*JGCR* 2(2), 1978, and its 1980 errata) was
  not available to check whether Swiss follows it.
- **Harrington.** The paper states no equinox.
  - J2000 reproduces Swiss exactly.
  - B1950 would be 0.70° east, and the equinox of 1988 0.17°.
  - Epoch: perihelion 1789 Aug 6 0h, JD 2374696.5.
- **Le Verrier.**
  - Equinox: the Comptes Rendus states 1847 Jan 1. Swiss latitudes are ≈ 0, which
    confirms an ecliptic orbit in that frame.
  - Gould (1850), restating the same memoir to the second (ϖ 284°5′48″ for 1800 + 39′20″;
    L 318°47′4″), changes the deltas by < 0.01°.
  - The remaining difference (eccentricity- or perihelion-like) points to another variant
    of Le Verrier's elements in Swiss, not identified.
- **Adams.** Hypothesis II with the equinox of the epoch matches far better than
  hypothesis I (degrees off) or the Oct 1845 communication. The residual is consistent
  with a slightly different a♅ or epoch.
- **Lowell.** Bower's "Longitude, 1914 Jul 0" read as the *true* heliocentric longitude
  gives 0.49°. Read as the mean longitude, the deviation is 16–33°.
  - Solution X2 does not match.
  - Lowell's "i 10°±" has no node, so the orbit lies in the ecliptic. Swiss latitudes are
    also ≈ 0.
- **Selena.** Globa's table advances 51°24′ a year, and 1990 → 1997 gains 3′. Both fit a
  period of 7 *Julian* years in *tropical* longitude, so the equinox is of date.
  - Distance from Kepler's law: 20.5 lunar distances. Swiss: "more than 20".
- **Waldemath.**
  - Sidereal period: 1/P = 1/177 + 1/365.242189, giving P = 119.22 d and 3.02°/day.
  - Kepler distance: 1.023 million km = 2.66 lunar distances. Swiss: 2.67 and "about 3°".
  - Epoch: at 1898 Feb 3 12h UT it sits on the Sun's geometric geocentric longitude from
    DE441, 314.7368°, as the predicted transit requires.
  - Waltemath's own element set was not found (only the MWR note), and the article gives
    no hour, so the epoch is uncertain by ±0.5 d, i.e. ±1.5°.
  - Low confidence: a reconstruction from the published period and transit.
